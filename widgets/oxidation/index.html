Thermal oxidation of silicon

The Deal–Grove model: how long does it take to grow an oxide, and why do thick oxides grow so slowly?

Key equations

The oxidant (O₂ or H₂O) diffuses through the oxide and reacts at the Si/SiO₂ interface. In steady state the two fluxes are equal, which gives the Deal–Grove relation

x_{ox}^2 + A\,x_{ox} = B\,(t+\tau), \qquad \tau = \frac{x_i^2 + A\,x_i}{B}
x_{ox}(t) = \frac{A}{2}\left[\sqrt{1+\frac{t+\tau}{A^2/4B}}-1\right]

Limits: x_{ox} \approx \tfrac{B}{A}(t+\tau) when t+\tau \ll A^2/4B (reaction-limited) and x_{ox} \approx \sqrt{B\,t} when t \gg A^2/4B (diffusion-limited).

Both constants follow Arrhenius laws, B = C_1 e^{-E_1/kT} and B/A = C_2 e^{-E_2/kT}:

AmbientC₁ (µm²/h)E₁ (eV)C₂ (µm/h)*E₂ (eV)
Dry O₂7.72 × 10²1.236.23 × 10⁶2.0
Wet H₂O3.86 × 10²0.781.63 × 10⁸2.05

* for (111) Si; divide C₂ by 1.68 for (100) Si. The oxide grows 46 % into the silicon: x_{Si} = 0.46\,x_{ox} (1 µm of Si becomes 2.17 µm of SiO₂; textbooks quote 0.44–0.46 depending on the assumed oxide density).

The original measurements of Deal & Grove (1965) were made on (111) Si; at 1200 °C they give B/A = 1.12\ \mathrm{\mu m/h} in dry O₂ and 14.4 µm/h in wet O₂ (water at 95 °C, 640 Torr H₂O), with activation energies 1.24 eV (dry) and 0.71 eV (wet) for B and 2.0 eV / 1.96 eV for B/A. The table above is the later fit used by Plummer et al.; its H₂O row agrees with Deal & Grove’s 640-Torr wet data within about 15 %.

Try it yourself

  1. How long does it take to grow 500 nm of wet oxide on a bare (100) wafer at 1100 °C? Calculate first, then check with the target slider.
    AnswerAt 1100 °C: B = 0.529\ \mathrm{\mu m^2/h}, B/A = 2.90\ \mathrm{\mu m/h}, A = 0.182\ \mathrm{\mu m}. t = (0.5^2 + 0.182\cdot0.5)/0.529 \approx 0.65\ \mathrm{h} \approx 39\ \mathrm{min}.
  2. A wafer already carries 100 nm of oxide. How much longer does it take to reach 200 nm in dry O₂ at 1000 °C than starting from bare silicon would take to reach 100 nm?
    Answer(100) Si: B = 0.0104\ \mathrm{\mu m^2/h}, A = 0.233\ \mathrm{\mu m}. Bare wafer (τ for the 25 nm offset = 0.62 h) to 100 nm: ≈ 2.6 h. From 100 nm (τ = 3.20 h) to 200 nm: ≈ 5.1 h – about twice as long for the same 100 nm, because the oxidant has to cross a thicker oxide.
  3. Why is it pointless to quote an oxidation time for 10 nm of dry oxide from this model?
    AnswerDeal–Grove does not describe the fast initial regime of dry oxidation (below ~25 nm). It absorbs it in the offset τ; thin gate oxides need other models or calibrated data.
  4. The colour of a wafer repeats as the oxide grows. Find two thicknesses below 600 nm that look blue. What is their difference, and why?
    AnswerRoughly 120 nm, 300 nm and 490 nm – a spacing of about 180–190 nm. The wafer looks blue mainly because yellow–orange light is removed: both reflections (air/SiO₂ and SiO₂/Si) flip the phase, so the reflectance has minima at 2nd = (m+\tfrac12)\lambda. A minimum at \lambda \approx 570\ \mathrm{nm} repeats every \Delta d = \lambda/(2n) \approx 570/(2\cdot1.46) \approx 195\ \mathrm{nm}.

Model & limitations

  • Rate constants are for 1 atm dry O₂ and for steam (the H₂O row of Plummer's Table 6-2, which agrees with Deal & Grove's 640-torr data within about 15 %) in the range ~800–1200 °C. Pressure, chlorine addition and heavy substrate doping change them. Real furnaces differ by tens of per cent – always calibrate.
  • Dry O₂: the model is shifted by τ corresponding to a 25 nm starting oxide (the textbook convention of Plummer et al.; Deal & Grove themselves adopted 23 nm). Below that the curve is not physical, and below ~950 °C the convention overestimates dry growth compared with measured furnace charts (e.g. the EPFL CMI oxidation charts: ≈ 1.5–1.7× at 900 °C, ≥ 2× at 800–850 °C and for short times).
  • The cross-section is drawn to scale with an automatic zoom in 1–2–5 steps (2 nm to 20 µm full height); the scale bar shows the current zoom.
  • The oxide colour is computed from thin-film interference at normal incidence in daylight; real colours depend on the light source, viewing angle and your display.
