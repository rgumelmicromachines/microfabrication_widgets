Vacuum: pump-down, conductance and outgassing

Evaporation, sputtering, plasma etching and ion implantation all need vacuum. Why does a big pump behind a thin pipe disappoint – and why does the last decade of pressure take hours?

Key equations

Pressure in a chamber of volume V pumped with the effective speed S_{eff} against the gas load Q:

V\frac{dp}{dt} = -S_{eff}\,p + Q, \qquad Q = 0:\ p = p_0\,e^{-S_{eff}t/V}, \qquad p_\infty = \frac{Q}{S_{eff}}

Speed at the chamber through a conductance C, and the molecular-flow conductance of a round pipe and an aperture (air, room temperature, cm and L/s):

\frac{1}{S_{eff}} = \frac{1}{S} + \frac{1}{C}, \qquad C_{pipe} = 12.1\,\frac{d^3}{l}, \qquad C_{aperture} = 11.6\,A

Mean free path, Knudsen number and flow regime (air, 22 °C):

\lambda = \frac{6.6\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Pa\cdot m}}{p}, \qquad Kn = \frac{\lambda}{d}:\ \ Kn < 0.01 \text{ viscous}, \quad Kn > 1 \text{ molecular}

Try it yourself

  1. A 500 L/s turbopump is connected through a pipe of 4 cm diameter and 50 cm length. What is the effective speed in molecular flow?
    AnswerC = 12.1\cdot 64/(50 + 5.3) \approx 14\ \mathrm{L/s} (with the entrance correction; 15.5 L/s without), S_{eff} = 1/(1/500 + 1/14) \approx 13.6\ \mathrm{L/s} – the pump is almost useless behind this pipe.
  2. How long does it take to pump a 50 L chamber with S_{eff} = 100\ \mathrm{L/s} from 1 Pa to 10⁻³ Pa, if only the gas in the volume counted?
    Answer\tau = V/S_{eff} = 0.5\ \mathrm{s}, t = \tau\ln(p_0/p) = 0.5\cdot\ln 1000 \approx 3.5\ \mathrm{s}. In reality the walls keep releasing gas, and the last decades take much longer.
  3. An unbaked stainless-steel chamber has 1 m² of wall; after 1 h of pumping it outgasses about 9 × 10⁻⁸ mbar·L/(s·cm²). Which pressure does S_{eff} = 100\ \mathrm{L/s} reach after 1 h and after 10 h?
    AnswerQ = 9\times10^{-8}\cdot10^4 = 9\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{mbar\,L/s} = 0.09\ \mathrm{Pa\,L/s}, p \approx Q/S_{eff} = 9\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{Pa} after 1 h; with Q \propto 1/t about 9\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{Pa} after 10 h.
  4. Mean free path and monolayer time at 10⁻⁴ Pa?
    Answer\lambda = 6.6\times10^{-3}/10^{-4} = 66\ \mathrm{m}; a clean surface is covered in about 2.5 s if every molecule sticks (O’Hanlon).

Model & limitations

  • Figure: the chamber (a cube) and the pipe are drawn to scale; the high-vacuum pump is a schematic turbomolecular pump of fixed size, whatever its speed.
  • High-vacuum stage only: pumping starts at 1 Pa after 5 min of roughing; the pump speed is constant (real pumps lose speed near their ultimate pressure and at their upper pressure limit). Air-like gas at room temperature.
  • Pipe conductance from Knudsen's interpolation formula (viscous to molecular), with Dushman's series combination as an approximate entrance correction for short pipes (it can be off by 10–15 % for intermediate l/d). Chamber treated as a cube; its own conductance and that of valves and traps are ignored.
  • Outgassing: one value per material after 1 h, falling as 1/t for unbaked metals and constant for baked steel. Published outgassing rates differ by more than an order of magnitude between sources, so the times are indicative. With a very small effective speed the 1/t law, extrapolated back to the first minutes, gives more gas than the pump removes, and the computed pressure first rises above the 1 Pa start: such a system would not have reached 1 Pa in 5 min of roughing. Elastomer seals, permeation and leaks are not included.
  • Flow-regime boundaries follow O’Hanlon (molecular for Kn > 1); other references put the molecular boundary at Kn > 0.5.
  • The molecules drawn in the chamber are schematic (their number falls with the logarithm of the pressure).
