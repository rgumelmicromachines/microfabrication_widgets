Sheet resistance and the four-point probe

Four needles, one current, one voltage: the quickest check of a doped layer or a metal film. Why four, what does 4.532 mean – and when is it wrong?

Key equations

Collinear probe with equal spacing s; current I through the outer pair, voltage V across the inner pair:

\text{thin infinite sheet: } R_s = \frac{\rho}{w} = \frac{\pi}{\ln 2}\frac{V}{I} = 4.532\,\frac{V}{I}, \qquad \text{semi-infinite bulk: } \rho = 2\pi s\,\frac{V}{I}

Finite thin circular slice of diameter d (probe centred) and thickness correction for a layer of thickness w:

R_s = C\,F\,\frac{V}{I}, \qquad C\!\left(\tfrac{d}{s}\right) = \frac{\pi}{\ln 2 + \ln\dfrac{(d/s)^2 + 3}{(d/s)^2 - 3}}, \qquad F\!\left(\tfrac{w}{s}\right) \to 1 \text{ for } w \ll s

Van der Pauw, any singly connected sample with four small contacts on its edge:

e^{-\pi R_A/R_s} + e^{-\pi R_B/R_s} = 1, \qquad R_A = R_B = R:\ R_s = \frac{\pi R}{\ln 2}

Try it yourself

  1. On a large wafer the probe reads 2.0 mV at 100 µA on an annealed implanted layer. What is the sheet resistance?
    AnswerR_s = 4.532\cdot 2.0\ \mathrm{mV}/100\ \mu\mathrm{A} = 4.532\cdot 20\ \Omega \approx 91\ \Omega/\square.
  2. The same layer, but on an 8 mm chip with a 1 mm probe: the probe again reads V/I = 20 Ω. What is R_s, and how wrong would 4.532·V/I be?
    Answerd/s = 8 \Rightarrow C = \pi/[\ln 2 + \ln(67/61)] = 3.99, so R_s = 3.99\cdot20 = 79.8\ \Omega/\square. 4.532·V/I = 90.6 Ω/□ would be 13.5 % too high.
  3. A 525 µm boron-doped wafer measures ρ = 1.49 Ω·cm. What is its doping, and what sheet resistance does the whole wafer have?
    AnswerFrom the Thurber curve: N \approx 1.0\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}. R_s = \rho/w = 1.49/0.0525\ \mathrm{cm} \approx 28\ \Omega/\square (w/s ≈ 0.33 for a 1.59 mm probe, so the thickness correction is negligible).
  4. A square van der Pauw structure gives R_A = R_B = 10\ \Omega. Sheet resistance?
    AnswerR_s = \pi\cdot10/\ln 2 = 45.3\ \Omega/\square.

Model & limitations

  • Point contacts, probe in the centre of a circular sample, uniform resistivity over the layer thickness and a non-conducting bottom (e.g. a layer isolated by a pn junction or on oxide). Real diffused layers have a doping profile; the four-point probe then measures the integrated sheet resistance.
  • For a finite, thick sample the corrections are combined as C·F, which is Smits' first-order approach; edge effects of an off-centre probe (Swartzendruber 1964) are not modelled.
  • Resistivity vs doping at 300 K from the fits of Thurber et al. (1981) for boron and phosphorus; other dopants (As, Sb) and compensated material differ. At 23 °C (ASTM F723) the values are 2–3 % lower at low doping.
  • No probe heating, minority-carrier injection or contact effects; the current recommendation is the ASTM F84 table (±20 %).
  • The van der Pauw example assumes a sample whose two resistances have the ratio set by the slider; the sketch of the sample is schematic.
