Spin coating: how thick is the resist?

Watch a full coating run – dispense, spread, spin-up, spin-off, edge bead removal – and see why spinning faster thins the film only with the square root of the speed.

Key relation

The empirical spin curve used in microfabrication courses:

t = k\,\frac{p^2}{\sqrt{w}}, \qquad t\ \text{in Å},\quad w = \frac{\text{spin speed in rpm}}{1000}
SymbolMeaningTypical value
kspinner constant (calibration)80–100
psolids content of the resist% by weight
wspin speed / 1000 rpm3–6 (3000–6000 rpm); spin time typically 30–60 s
tfilm thickness1–2 µm in Si processes

Consequences: t \propto 1/\sqrt{w} – twice the speed gives a \sqrt2 thinner film; t \propto p^2 – the resist formulation sets the range. The soft bake thins the film by up to about 25 %; the edge bead can be 20–30 times thicker than the film.

Try it yourself

  1. A resist with 25 % solids and a spinner with k = 90: which speed gives a 2.5 µm film?
    Answer\sqrt{w} = k p^2 / t = 90\cdot 625 / 25\,000 = 2.25, so w = 5.06, i.e. about 5060 rpm. Check it in Explore mode.
  2. Your resist (20 %, k = 90) gives 1.8 µm at 4000 rpm, but you need 1.2 µm. What speed would that take, and what is the better solution?
    Answerw = 4\cdot(1.8/1.2)^2 = 9, i.e. 9000 rpm – outside the usual 3000–6000 rpm. Better: a thinner resist. At 4000 rpm, p = 20\,\% \cdot \sqrt{1.2/1.8} \approx 16.3\,\% gives 1.2 µm.
  3. How high can the edge bead of a 1.8 µm film be, and why does it matter for contact printing?
    AnswerUp to 20–30 × 1.8 µm ≈ 36–54 µm. The ridge keeps the mask that far from the resist over the rest of the wafer – the contact becomes a proximity gap and the resolution drops. Hence edge bead removal right after coating.

Model & limitations

  • The spin curve is an empirical rule of thumb, not a physical law. Its units are not printed in the sources; the thickness comes out in ångströms, the only reading consistent with the typical values (3000–6000 rpm → 1–2 µm).
  • The robust part is the scaling with speed, t \propto w^{-1/2}, which holds for low- and medium-viscosity resists. Thick, viscous resists (e.g. SU-8) follow a steeper law, closer to w^{-1}.
  • The absolute thickness depends on everything lumped into k – spin time, acceleration, solvent, exhaust, temperature and humidity. Use a measured spin curve for your resist and spinner.
  • The soft-bake shrinkage (up to ~25 %) and the edge-bead height (up to 20–30 × t) are typical upper values; the drawing of the edge bead is not to scale.
  • The coating run is a schematic animation of the recipe set in the panel (all speeds, times, the acceleration and the edge bead removal can be changed under Spin recipe); the default is a typical recipe (dispense at rest, 8 s spread at 500 rpm, ramp to the final speed, 60 s spin, edge bead removal with a solvent jet at about 1000 rpm, then 15 s spin dry at about 2000 rpm – both below the coating speed so the film does not thin further). It is a time-lapse – real time while dispensing, 2× while spreading, 8× during the long spin, 3× for edge bead removal, 4× for the spin dry – and the rotation is drawn 50× slower than at 4000 rpm. The thinning during the spin only illustrates the shape of the Emslie–Bonner–Peck solution for a liquid film, h ∝ 1/√(1 + t/τ) with τ ∝ 1/ω² (fast at first, then slower), followed by drying; the final thickness comes from the spin curve.
