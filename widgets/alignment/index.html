Alignment and overlay: shift, rotation and run-out

Every mask after the first has to land on the pattern that is already on the wafer. What can the operator correct at two alignment marks, and what has to be absorbed by the layout?

Key equations

Linear overlay model

The new pattern is shifted by (T_x, T_y), rotated by a small angle \theta about the wafer centre and scaled by 1 + M against the pattern on the wafer. The overlay error at the position (x, y) is

\Delta x = T_x - \theta\,y + M\,x, \qquad \Delta y = T_y + \theta\,x + M\,y

The rotation term is the first-order expansion of a rigid rotation, x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta \approx x - \theta y. On a wafer of radius R the largest error lies at the edge:

|\Delta|_{max} = \sqrt{T_x^2 + T_y^2} + R\,\sqrt{\theta^2 + M^2}

Two alignment marks

With the marks at (\mp L/2,\ 0) the four residuals give the four parameters:

T = \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta_L + \Delta_R), \qquad \theta = \frac{\Delta y_R - \Delta y_L}{L}, \qquad M = \frac{\Delta x_R - \Delta x_L}{L}

A reading error of \pm\delta at each mark leaves a rotation of up to 2\delta/L unnoticed.

Enclosure and error budget

A pad that overlaps its contact window by the enclosure e on every side covers it while |\Delta x| \le e and |\Delta y| \le e. Independent random contributions \sigma_i combine as

\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 + \dots}

because the variances of independent quantities add. Systematic errors of the same sign add directly.

Try it yourself

  1. A mask is rotated by 30 µrad against a 100 mm wafer. How large is that angle in degrees, and what is the overlay error at the wafer edge?
    Answer30\ \mathrm{\mu rad} = 0.0017^\circ. At the edge: 30\times10^{-6}\cdot 50\ \mathrm{mm} = 1.5\ \mathrm{\mu m}.
  2. Two marks are 70 mm apart. The operator reads \Delta x = +0.1, \Delta y = -0.4\ \mathrm{\mu m} at the left mark and \Delta x = +0.5, \Delta y = +0.3\ \mathrm{\mu m} at the right one. Find shift, rotation and run-out.
    AnswerT = (+0.3,\ -0.05)\ \mathrm{\mu m}, \theta = 0.7\ \mathrm{\mu m}/70\ \mathrm{mm} = 10\ \mathrm{\mu rad}, M = 0.4\ \mathrm{\mu m}/70\ \mathrm{mm} = 5.7\ \mathrm{ppm}. Shift and rotation can be removed on the stage; the run-out stays.
  3. A soda-lime mask and the wafer are both 2 K warmer than at the first exposure. What is the run-out, and how far off is a die 45 mm from the centre after perfect alignment of shift and rotation? Repeat for fused silica.
    AnswerSoda-lime: M = (9.06 - 2.6)\cdot 2 = 12.9\ \mathrm{ppm}, 12.9\times10^{-6}\cdot 45\ \mathrm{mm} = 0.58\ \mathrm{\mu m}. Fused silica: M = (0.54 - 2.6)\cdot 2 = -4.1\ \mathrm{ppm}, 0.19 µm in the opposite direction. The mask that expands least is not the one that fits silicon best.
  4. The marks can be set to ±0.5 µm each. Which rotation can remain unnoticed with the marks 60 mm apart, and which with 20 mm? What does each mean at the edge of a 100 mm wafer?
    Answer60 mm: 2\cdot0.5/60 = 17\ \mathrm{\mu rad}, 0.83 µm at the edge. 20 mm: 50 µrad, 2.5 µm at the edge.
  5. An overlay budget has three independent random contributions of 0.5, 0.5 and 0.65 µm and one systematic run-out of 1.0 µm at the wafer edge. What enclosure covers them?
    AnswerRandom part: \sqrt{0.5^2 + 0.5^2 + 0.65^2} = 0.96\ \mathrm{\mu m}. The systematic error adds directly: about 2.0 µm.

Model & limitations

  • The overlay model is the standard linear one: shift, a small rotation about the wafer centre and one scale factor. It leaves out different scales in x and y, skew, distortion of the mask or of the wafer (for example bow after film deposition) and every higher-order term. The formula for the largest error follows from the model; it was derived for this page and not taken from a textbook.
  • Thermal run-out assumes that mask and wafer are at the same temperature, both ΔT away from the temperature at which the first layer was printed, and that both expand freely. A mask heated by the lamp or a wafer clamped on a chuck behaves differently.
  • The expansion coefficients of the glasses are mean values for 20–300 °C from manufacturer data; near room temperature they are slightly lower. The value for soda-lime glass varies with its composition.
  • Typical figures for a manual contact aligner, not modelled as random numbers here: alignment accuracy below 0.5 µm in the specification table of one mask aligner (SUSS MJB4), while its brochure text says 1 µm; verniers are read to about ±0.5 µm.
  • The pad covers the window when the window lies fully under it. Whether a partly covered window still gives a good contact depends on the process and is not judged.
  • The dies are evaluated at their centres on a 10 mm grid out to 45 mm from the wafer centre.
  • The marks (frame with a 6 µm opening, 4 µm square) and the vernier (pitches 4.0 and 3.5 µm, reading in 0.5 µm steps) are examples, drawn to scale in their own frames. The contact window is 6 µm wide; pad and window are to scale. The arrows on the wafer map are enlarged by the factor given under the map.
  • The root-sum-square rule holds for independent random contributions. It is a statement of statistics (variances add); which contributions of a real tool are independent has to be established for that tool.
