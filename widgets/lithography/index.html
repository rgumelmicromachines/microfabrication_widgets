Optical lithography – resolution limits

Light diffracts at every mask edge. How small can we print – in contact, with a gap, and through a lens?

Key equations

Contact and proximity printing

For a grating of lines and spaces of width b, gap s and resist thickness d, the theoretical limit is

b_{min} \approx 1.5\,\sqrt{\lambda\left(s + \tfrac{d}{2}\right)}

In practice the prefactor is closer to 2.5 (DTU Nanolab lithography notes, after Madou). Some textbooks use the simpler form \sqrt{\lambda g} – the conventions differ by tens of per cent, so always state which one you use.

Projection printing

R = k_1\,\frac{\lambda}{NA}, \qquad \mathrm{DOF} \approx \pm k_2\,\frac{\lambda}{NA^2},\ k_2 \approx 0.5

A periodic pattern of pitch p diffracts into orders at \sin\theta_m = m\lambda/p. An image needs at least the 0th and one 1st order inside the pupil, which gives the smallest pitch

p_{min} = \frac{\lambda}{NA\,(1+\sigma)} \quad\Rightarrow\quad k_{1,\min} = \frac{0.5}{1+\sigma} \ge 0.25

Try it yourself

  1. Your lab mask aligner uses i-line light and 1.4 µm thick resist. What is the smallest line width you can expect in hard contact, and with a 10 µm proximity gap?
    AnswerContact (s = 0): 1.5\sqrt{0.365\cdot0.7} \approx 0.76\ \mathrm{\mu m}. Proximity 10 µm: 1.5\sqrt{0.365\cdot10.7} \approx 3.0\ \mathrm{\mu m}. Check both in Explore mode.
  2. An i-line stepper has NA = 0.5 and σ = 0.5. What is the smallest half-pitch it can image at all? What k₁ does that correspond to?
    Answerp_{min} = 0.365/(0.5\cdot1.5) = 0.487\ \mathrm{\mu m}, half-pitch 0.243 µm, k_1 = 0.243\cdot0.5/0.365 = 0.33. Below it the image is perfectly flat.
  3. Set projection, ArF, NA = 0.85, half-pitch 120 nm. Increase the defocus until the lines no longer print. Compare with \pm\lambda/(2NA^2).
    Answer\lambda/(2NA^2) = 0.193/(2\cdot0.7225) \approx 0.13\ \mathrm{\mu m}. In the simulation (dose 2.5) the printed width is 106 nm in focus, −5 % at 0.10 µm and −12 % at 0.13 µm; nothing prints from about 0.20 µm. A ±10 % width budget therefore gives about ±0.12 µm – close to the estimate.
  4. In projection mode, change the dose. Why does the printed space width change although the mask does not?
    AnswerThe aerial image has sloped edges. A higher dose lowers the threshold E_0/E, so the resist clears further into the slope and the space widens. The steeper the edge (higher contrast), the less sensitive the width is to dose.

Model & limitations

  • Scalar diffraction of an infinitely thin, ideal chrome mask with equal lines and spaces (Kirchhoff approximation). Polarisation, mask 3-D effects and lens aberrations are ignored – these matter at high NA.
  • Projection imaging uses Abbe's method: the image is the average over points of a circular source of radius σ·NA. Dimensions are given at the wafer.
  • Proximity printing propagates the field through air over s + d/2 (the middle of the resist), averaged over the illumination cone. Refraction and absorption in the resist, standing waves and the substrate reflection are ignored.
  • The resist is a sharp threshold: positive resist clears wherever the dose exceeds the dose-to-clear E_0. Real resists have finite contrast, so the walls are sloped.
  • Defocus is applied with the vacuum wavelength; inside a resist of refractive index n the effective defocus is roughly n times smaller.
  • Illumination is monochromatic. For an ideal periodic grating, proximity printing shows the Talbot effect: the image contrast drops and recovers (partly reversed) with the gap, with a period of 2p^2/\lambda. Broadband mercury light and finite mask patterns weaken this in practice.
  • The illumination half-angle of real mask aligners is typically a few degrees and is set by the exposure optics.
