Evaporation and sputtering: line of sight

In a good vacuum the atoms fly straight from the source to the wafer. That decides how uniform the film is, why the far edge gets less – and why lift-off works with evaporation but not with sputtering.

Key equations

Thickness on a flat wafer parallel to a small source at distance h, at radius r from the point above the source:

\text{point source: } \frac{t}{t_0} = \left[1 + (r/h)^2\right]^{-3/2}, \qquad \text{small surface source: } \frac{t}{t_0} = \left[1 + (r/h)^2\right]^{-2}

The surface source emits according to Knudsen's cosine law, \propto \cos\varphi. A cosine source collects the fraction R^2/(R^2+h^2) of its vapour on a wafer of radius R. Source and wafers on one sphere receive the same thickness everywhere – the planetary dome.

Mean free path of gas molecules of diameter d and the fraction that crosses a distance h without a collision:

\lambda = \frac{k_B T}{\sqrt{2}\,\pi d^2 p}, \qquad f_0 = e^{-h/\lambda}

For air at room temperature \lambda \approx 0.66\ \mathrm{cm\cdot Pa}/p: 6.6 cm at 0.1 Pa, 6.6 m at 10⁻³ Pa. Shadowing behind a step of height s: offset s\tan\theta with \tan\theta = r/h.

Try it yourself

  1. A small surface source evaporates onto a 150 mm wafer. How far away must the wafer be for the edge to get at least 95 % of the centre thickness? Which fraction of the vapour lands on the wafer then?
    Answer[1 + (R/h)^2]^{-2} \ge 0.95 \Rightarrow R/h \le \sqrt{0.95^{-1/2} - 1} = 0.161, so h \ge 7.5/0.161 \approx 47\ \mathrm{cm}. Collected: R^2/(R^2+h^2) \approx 2.5\ \% – the rest coats the chamber.
  2. What is the mean free path in air at 10⁻³ Pa (22 °C), and which fraction of the atoms crosses 40 cm without a collision? And in argon at 0.5 Pa?
    Answer\lambda = 0.66/10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm} \approx 6.6\ \mathrm{m}, e^{-40/656} \approx 94\ \%. In Ar at 0.5 Pa, \lambda \approx 1.4\ \mathrm{cm} and practically no atom (e^{-29} \approx 10^{-13}) arrives without a collision.
  3. The edge of a 200 mm wafer, 50 cm above the source, carries a 1.5 µm resist step. At which angle do the atoms arrive, and how large is the shadow?
    Answer\theta = \arctan(10/50) = 11.3^\circ, shadow 1.5\ \mu\mathrm{m}\cdot 0.2 = 0.30\ \mu\mathrm{m}. Patterns at the wafer edge shift by that much; the overhang of a lift-off resist must be larger than this offset so that the film still breaks.

Model & limitations

  • Ideal small source (point or cosine) and a flat, stationary wafer parallel to it; every arriving atom sticks. Real sources have a finite size, e-beam hearths are often more forward-directed (cosnφ with n > 1; Graper 1973), and wafers are usually rotated or placed on a planetary dome.
  • The mean free path uses hard-sphere diameters from gas viscosity (air 3.74 Å, Ar 3.67 Å) at 20 °C. The collision-free fraction e^{-h/\lambda} is for straight paths through a uniform gas.
  • The scattered paths drawn in the chamber and the coverage of the lift-off step are schematic: they only show that scattering destroys the directionality. Real sputter step coverage depends on the tool, pressure, bias and target–substrate distance.
  • The thickness and the shadow are pure geometry; deposition rate (vapour pressure of the source, Hertz–Knudsen flux), film structure and stress are not modelled.
