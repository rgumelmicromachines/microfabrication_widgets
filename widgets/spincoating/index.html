Spin coating: how thick is the resist?

Watch a full coating run – dispense, spread, spin-up, spin-off, edge bead removal – and see why spinning faster thins the film only with the square root of the speed.

Key relation

The empirical spin curve used in microfabrication courses:

t = k\,\frac{p^2}{\sqrt{w}}, \qquad t\ \text{in Å},\quad w = \frac{\text{spin speed in rpm}}{1000}
SymbolMeaningTypical value
kspinner constant (calibration)measured per resist and spinner (90 here)
psolids content of the resist% by weight
wspin speed / 1000 rpm3–6 (3000–6000 rpm); spin time some tens of seconds
tfilm thickness≈ 1–2 µm for the defaults here

Consequences: t \propto 1/\sqrt{w} – twice the speed gives a \sqrt2 thinner film; t \propto p^2 – the resist formulation sets the range. The soft bake drives off the remaining solvent (roughly 10–35 % right after coating), so the film thins further; at the wafer edge the resist piles up into an edge bead, typically about as high as the film and several mm wide.

Try it yourself

  1. A resist with 25 % solids and a spinner with k = 90: which speed gives a 2.5 µm film?
    Answer\sqrt{w} = k p^2 / t = 90\cdot 625 / 25\,000 = 2.25, so w = 5.06, i.e. about 5060 rpm. Check it in Explore mode.
  2. Your resist (20 %, k = 90) gives 1.8 µm at 4000 rpm, but you need 1.2 µm. What speed would that take, and what is the better solution?
    Answerw = 4\cdot(1.8/1.2)^2 = 9, i.e. 9000 rpm – outside the usual 3000–6000 rpm. Better: a thinner resist. At 4000 rpm, p = 20\,\% \cdot \sqrt{1.2/1.8} \approx 16.3\,\% gives 1.2 µm.
  3. The edge bead of a 1.8 µm film rises another ≈ 1.8 µm. In contact printing with the i-line (365 nm) the mask rests on that ridge. Estimate the smallest printable feature in the wafer centre with and without the edge bead, using b_{min} = 1.5\sqrt{\lambda\,(s + d/2)} (see the lithography lesson).
    AnswerHard contact, s = 0: 1.5\sqrt{0.365\cdot0.9} \approx 0.86\ \mathrm{\mu m}. On the bead, s \approx 1.8\ \mathrm{\mu m}: 1.5\sqrt{0.365\cdot2.7} \approx 1.49\ \mathrm{\mu m} – the resolution is almost halved. Thick resists have much higher beads. Hence edge bead removal right after coating.

Model & limitations

  • The spin curve is an empirical rule of thumb, not a physical law. It is a classroom form of the fitted law t = K C^{\beta}\eta^{\gamma}/\omega^{\alpha} (Madou 2011); its units are usually not printed – here t is in ångströms, and k = 90 is illustrative.
  • The robust part is the scaling with speed, t \propto w^{-1/2}, which holds for low- and medium-viscosity resists. Thick, viscous resists (e.g. SU-8) follow a steeper law, closer to w^{-1}.
  • The absolute thickness depends on everything lumped into k – spin time, acceleration, solvent, exhaust, temperature and humidity. Use a measured spin curve for your resist and spinner.
  • The soft-bake thinning (−25 %) is illustrative: right after coating the film holds roughly 10–35 % solvent, depending on the resist and its thickness (MicroChemicals). The edge bead is drawn about as high as the film, typical for thin resists (DTU Nanolab); thick or viscous resists form much higher beads.
  • The coating run is a schematic animation of the recipe set in the panel (all speeds, times, the acceleration and the edge bead removal can be changed under Spin recipe); the default is a typical recipe (dispense at rest, 8 s spread at 500 rpm, ramp to the final speed, 60 s spin, edge bead removal with a solvent jet at about 1000 rpm, then 15 s spin dry at about 2000 rpm – both below the coating speed, as usual). It is a time-lapse – real time while dispensing, 2× while spreading, 8× during the long spin, 3× for edge bead removal, 4× for the spin dry – and the rotation is drawn 50× slower than at 4000 rpm. The thinning during the spin only illustrates the shape of the Emslie–Bonner–Peck solution for a liquid film, h ∝ 1/√(1 + t/τ) with τ ∝ 1/ω² (fast at first, then slower), followed by drying; the final thickness comes from the spin curve.
