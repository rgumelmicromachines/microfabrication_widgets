Negative resist: what the light hits, stays

Exposure and a bake cross-link the resin, and the developer removes the rest. The resist does not bleach, so the light fades with depth: thick films need far more dose, and the weakly exposed resist near the substrate gives an undercut.

Key equations

Without bleaching the absorption stays constant during the exposure, and the dose at depth z follows the Beer–Lambert law with the penetration depth \delta = 1/\alpha:

E(z) = E\,e^{-z/\delta}

Model of this page: after a bake of completeness \kappa the resist is cross-linked, and insoluble, where \kappa E(z) \ge E_{gel}. Under a large exposed area that holds down to

z_x = \delta\,\ln\frac{\kappa E}{E_{gel}}, \qquad \text{so a film of thickness } d \text{ needs } E \ge \frac{E_{gel}}{\kappa}\,e^{d/\delta}

Each additional penetration depth of thickness multiplies the dose by e \approx 2.7.

Try it yourself

  1. The penetration depth is 1.5 µm. Which share of the surface dose reaches the bottom of a 3 µm film, and of a 6 µm film?
    Answere^{-3/1.5} = 14\ \% and e^{-6/1.5} = 1.8\ \%.
  2. How many gel doses cross-link a 3 µm film down to the substrate (δ = 1.5 µm, complete bake), and how many a 5 µm film?
    Answere^{3/1.5} = 7.4 and e^{5/1.5} = 28. Two more micrometres cost almost four times the dose.
  3. A film is exposed with 8 gel doses, but the bake only reaches κ = 0.5. How deep is it cross-linked (δ = 1.5 µm)?
    Answerz_x = 1.5\ \mathrm{\mu m}\cdot\ln(0.5\cdot 8) = 2.1\ \mathrm{\mu m}. A 3 µm film then stands on soluble resist.
  4. In Explore mode, expose a 3 µm film with 8 and with 60 gel doses. Why is the undercut similar although the lines are much wider at the higher dose?
    AnswerThe dose shifts the whole boundary of the cross-linked region outwards. The undercut comes mainly from the ratio of the dose at the top to the dose at the bottom, e^{d/\delta}, which does not depend on the dose; the shape of the image at the line edge changes it a little.

Model & limitations

  • One sharp threshold, the gel dose, separates soluble from cross-linked resist, and the developer does not attack cross-linked resist at all. A real resist passes through weakly cross-linked states that erode in the developer, so real edges are less sharp and thin lines lose some height after a low dose or a cool bake.
  • Doses are given in gel doses and development times in units of the time that clears an unexposed film. No gel dose in mJ/cm² and no development rate were found for a specific resist; take both from the data sheet of the resist you use.
  • The bake is one number, the completeness κ of the cross-linking. The application notes give the trend (hotter or longer: more cross-linking; typically 110–125 °C for 1–2 minutes) but no kinetics. Thermal cross-linking of unexposed resist in a bake that is too hot, which slows the development, is not modelled.
  • The penetration depth of 1–2 µm is the range given for the AZ nLOF 2000 series at the i-line. Chemically amplified resists for very thick films absorb far less and are not described by this range.
  • The mask image is that of equal lines and spaces in hard contact, with diffraction calculated for one plane in the middle of the resist; it does not change with depth. The small ripples at the bottom of the cross-linked region are the diffraction ripples of that image. Scattering in the resist and reflection from the substrate are left out.
  • The development front moves at one rate through soluble resist (eikonal equation) on a grid of 41 × 33 points. The cross-section is to scale; the mask and the light arrows are schematic.
